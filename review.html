Detailed Review and Classification

Comparing approaches to agent-based urban traffic simulation and congestion reduction.

Review Overview

The detailed reviews are available for all 20 shortlisted papers. Each review examines the modeling approach, system components, important configuration choices, supporting evidence, limitations, and relevance to urban traffic congestion. The cross-paper comparison brings these findings together to identify shared challenges and differences between approaches.

All papers are classified according to their main research contribution: travel behavior and route choice, traffic control and coordination, travel-demand management, simulation frameworks and computational efficiency, or calibration and validation methods. Each paper has one primary category and a specific focus. Connections between categories are discussed within the reviews and comparisons.

These categories describe what a study contributes rather than simply which software or algorithm it uses. For example, RouteRL is a framework centered on route-choice behavior, while the automated-vehicle corridor study examines capacity allocation rather than an adaptive signal controller. Travel-demand management remains as a separate category because it evaluates policies designed to influence when, where, or how people travel.

The configuration choices are interpreted according to their role: scenario inputs define the network and demand, behavioral and control settings govern decisions and interactions, and computational settings govern simulation or learning. The reported values describe the authors’ experiments rather than universal settings for other cities. Any calibrated values and optimization results are distinguished from initial inputs.

Travel Behavior and Route Choice

Studies in this category examine how travelers organize daily activities, select destinations and routes, respond to traffic conditions, and collectively influence congestion.

An Agent-Based Microsimulation Model of Swiss Travel: First Results

Raney, B. et al. 2003. An Agent-Based Microsimulation Model of Swiss Travel: First Results. Networks and Spatial Economics 3, 1 (2003), 23–41. https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022096916806 .

View the abstract summary and brief comment

Raney and colleagues examine how individual route choices contribute to congestion across a large transportation network. Their framework connects route planning, traffic simulation, and learning from previous journeys. This creates a feedback process: travelers choose routes based on expected travel times, their combined choices determine where congestion develops, and the resulting delays influence their next choices. Each traveler stores alternative routes and their experienced travel times. During each simulation cycle, 10% of travelers receive newly calculated routes based on updated traffic conditions, while the others choose among routes they have already tried. The experiments repeat this process for 50 cycles. This represents adaptation between journeys, rather than drivers changing direction during a trip.

The model represents roads as queues in which vehicles leave in the order they arrive. Each road has a minimum travel time and limited space, so a full road can block traffic entering from upstream. This captures how congestion spreads without simulating every lane change or interaction between neighboring vehicles. The simplified representation allows the researchers to simulate approximately one million morning trips across a Swiss network containing more than 28,000 road links. However, the tested model covers car travel using estimated trips between origins and destinations; it does not yet include the broader activity planning and transportation choices discussed in the framework. Its computational results also show that simplifying vehicle movement addresses only part of the efficiency problem. On the reported hardware, traffic simulation takes about four minutes per cycle, while routing, feedback, and database operations take about 45 minutes.

Configuring the system requires an origin, destination, and departure time for each traveler, together with road-link lengths, free-flow speeds, and capacities. The agent database connects route planning with traffic simulation by retaining alternative routes and their experienced travel times. Simulated link travel times are returned to the router in 15-minute intervals, allowing route costs to vary across the day. The 10% replanning fraction and 50 simulation cycles describe adaptation between journeys, while road capacities and storage limits govern movement within each journey. Route-choice sensitivity is another behavioral setting because it determines how strongly travelers favor previously faster routes. Reproducing this approach therefore requires configuring both the transportation network and the feedback process, rather than representing vehicle movement alone.

The experiments examine both the effects of route adaptation and agreement with observed traffic. In the Gotthard scenario, 50,000 vehicles travel toward a common destination. As travelers gain experience, they spread across more routes, queues become shorter overall, and arrivals occur earlier than in the initial simulation. This illustrates how individual learning can change congestion patterns without directly coordinating every traveler. In the Switzerland experiment, the authors compare simulated traffic volumes with observed counts and results from VISUM, a transportation modeling system. The agent-based model achieves a mean relative error of 25.38%, compared with 30.42% for VISUM. This supports its ability to reproduce traffic volumes in that comparison, although it does not establish the accuracy of every modeled behavior.

Several limitations affect how these results should be interpreted. The demand estimates had already been adjusted using traffic-count information, so the comparison does not clearly establish performance against independent observations. Matching road traffic volumes also does not necessarily mean that individual travel times or route preferences are accurate. Gridlock around Zurich exposes another difficulty: unrealistic congestion may arise from the estimated demand, road capacities, or the simulation rules, and the authors could not fully separate these explanations. They adjusted network capacities but acknowledged uncertainty about the underlying problem. Similarly, the selected replanning rate and number of cycles provide a practical experimental procedure without guaranteeing that route choices have stabilized.

For our survey, this paper establishes a useful foundation for studying congestion through the interaction of individual travel decisions. Its experiments show how learning can redistribute traffic, while also demonstrating why improvements in simulated congestion must be assessed alongside data quality and modeling assumptions. The framework could support investigations of how travelers respond to changes in capacity or route guidance, but the reported results do not establish a universally effective congestion strategy. Its separation of route planning, road simulation, and behavioral feedback also offers a useful conceptual reference for future DEVSJAVA work, although DEVSJAVA is not used in the paper.

Generating complete all-day activity plans with genetic algorithms

Charypar, D. and Nagel, K. 2005. Generating complete all-day activity plans with genetic algorithms. Transportation 32, 4 (2005), 369–397. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11116-004-8287-y .

View the abstract summary and brief comment

Traffic demand begins with the activities people need or want to complete throughout the day. Charypar and Nagel address this part of transportation modeling by developing a genetic algorithm that generates complete daily activity plans for individual travelers. Rather than starting with a fixed collection of trips, their approach selects activities, arranges their order, chooses locations, and allocates time. Travel then connects activities performed at different places. This provides a foundation for studying how work schedules, shopping, childcare, and leisure collectively create demand on a transportation network.

The algorithm maintains several competing plans for each traveler and gradually improves them through crossover, mutation, and selection. Crossover combines parts of two existing plans, while mutation changes elements such as activity order, location, or duration. A utility function scores each plan by balancing the benefits of performing activities against travel time, waiting, and scheduling penalties. Opening hours and other timing restrictions influence which arrangements are worthwhile. An important modeling detail is that travel consumes time that could otherwise be spent on activities, so its effective cost includes both the direct travel penalty and the lost opportunity to do something else.

Setting up the model requires the activities available to an individual, possible activity locations, opening hours, preferred durations, and scheduling constraints. A candidate plan records activity participation, order, location, and timing. The genetic algorithm’s population consists of alternative schedules for one traveler, rather than a population of different travelers. Important configuration choices include the utility coefficients, mutation settings, number of candidate plans, and search duration. The longer experiments use 300 candidate plans and 10 million generations, while the faster experiments use 50 plans and 200,000 generations. These configurations illustrate the trade-off between computation time and consistency of the resulting schedules. Connecting the method to congestion analysis would require an additional feedback process that replaces distance-based travel-time estimates with updated costs from a traffic simulation.

The experiments examine three illustrative activity sets representing a busy working day, a nonworking day with household responsibilities, and a more flexible retirement schedule. Longer runs generally produce consistent activity patterns and location choices, while shorter runs test whether useful plans can be generated with less computation. On the reported hardware, the longer configuration takes approximately 140–230 seconds per planning run, compared with 3–5 seconds for the shorter configuration. In the busy-day example, three of five shorter runs recover the same activity pattern and locations as the longer runs, although their timing is less refined. These results demonstrate the method’s flexibility, but also show that reducing computation can make the resulting schedules less consistent.

The evaluation primarily establishes that the algorithm can find high-scoring plans under its specified assumptions; it does not establish that those plans reproduce observed human schedules. The authors acknowledge that the utility parameters require further development and that different travelers are unlikely to share one set of preferences. Some generated plans also expose missing behavioral constraints, including an example that schedules collecting children without first taking them to kindergarten. Travel times are simplified estimates based on distance rather than outcomes from a congested network, and coordinated planning across households or multiple days remains outside the reported tests. These limitations distinguish successful optimization from convincing behavioral validation.

The paper strengthens the demand-generation side of congestion research by explaining how trips emerge from connected daily decisions. Changes in travel conditions could affect not only route choice, but also activity locations, departure times, and the time available for other commitments. This complements Raney’s route-adaptation framework by introducing decisions that occur before a route is selected. However, congestion avoidance through feedback from a traffic simulation is proposed as a future extension rather than demonstrated in these experiments. The method is therefore best understood as a flexible planning component that could support the evaluation of congestion policies once connected to a traffic model and calibrated against travel behavior.

RouteRL: Multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for urban route choice with autonomous vehicles

Akman, A. O. et al. 2025. RouteRL: Multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for urban route choice with autonomous vehicles. SoftwareX 31, Article 102279 (Sep. 2025). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.softx.2025.102279 .

View the abstract summary and brief comment

Akman and colleagues introduce RouteRL as a framework for studying route choices when human drivers and autonomous vehicles share the same road network. Human travelers follow behavioral learning models, while autonomous vehicles use multi-agent reinforcement learning to pursue specified objectives. These objectives can favor individual travel time, cooperation within a group, or wider system performance. The framework makes it possible to examine an important congestion problem: an automated routing strategy can benefit its own users while increasing delays for other travelers.

The model separates route decisions from the simulation of vehicle movement. Each traveler receives an origin, destination, departure time, and a discrete set of candidate routes generated through JanuX. Agents choose routes before departure, and SUMO simulates the resulting traffic using microscopic vehicle interactions. An agent receives its reward after reaching its destination, with travel-time information subsequently used to update its behavior. The software organizes this process into environment management, human learning, and supporting services for recording and visualization. Human behavior can be represented using the Gawron, CULO, or WeightedAverage models, allowing the same traffic environment to be examined under different assumptions about adaptation.

Setting up an experiment requires selecting the road network, travel demand, available routes, human learning model, autonomous-vehicle share, and reward objective. The illustrative experiments begin with 100 simulated days of human learning, introduce autonomous agents, train their policies, and evaluate them over 100 testing episodes. Neural-network and training settings vary by algorithm and scenario. For example, the Cologne experiment using Value Decomposition Networks reports a policy-network depth of two with 256 units per layer, two candidate routes, a learning rate of 0.003, a discount factor of 0.99, and a replay memory of 1,000 experiences. These values belong to that particular experiment and should not be treated as a universal RouteRL configuration.

Experiments on Cologne, Ingolstadt, and Manhattan networks demonstrate how the framework can compare objectives and learning algorithms. In one Cologne example, 40 of 100 human drivers become autonomous agents with an objective that disadvantages the other group. The resulting route choices allow autonomous agents to travel faster while human travel times increase. This is useful evidence that improved performance for a fleet does not necessarily imply less congestion for everyone. It also shows why an evaluation should report outcomes separately for different traveler groups alongside network-wide measures.

RouteRL provides a practical setting for investigating how congestion emerges from repeated interactions between routing strategies. However, each agent repeats a single origin–destination trip, and routes remain fixed during each journey. The experiments therefore do not represent complete daily activity schedules or adaptation while traveling. Human behavioral models also approximate decisions rather than reproduce every source of variation in real travelers. The framework’s main contribution is a configurable environment for controlled comparisons, with the effectiveness of a congestion-reduction strategy depending on its reward design, behavioral assumptions, and effects on users outside the optimized group.

Social implications of coexistence of CAVs and human drivers in the context of route choice

Jamróz, G. et al. 2025. Social implications of coexistence of CAVs and human drivers in the context of route choice. Scientific Reports 15, Article 6768 (Feb. 2025). https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-90783-w .

View the abstract summary and brief comment

Jamróz and colleagues examine how coordinated routing by connected and autonomous vehicles affects human drivers sharing the same roads. Their central concern is the distribution of benefits: a fleet may reduce its own travel time without improving conditions for everyone. The model compares fleet objectives that prioritize its users, favor human drivers, pursue overall efficiency, or deliberately disadvantage other travelers. This makes the study useful for examining whose interests a congestion-management strategy serves.

The system contains one origin–destination pair connected by two independent routes. Human agents maintain travel-time estimates and update only the route they experienced that day. Their decisions combine these estimates with individual route preferences and occasional exploration. A central fleet operator then allocates autonomous vehicles between the routes according to its objective, assuming perfect prediction of the human drivers’ route totals. Once all choices are made, congestion-dependent travel-time functions determine the outcomes, which feed into the next day’s decisions. Vehicle movement is therefore represented through aggregate route costs rather than individual car-following or lane-changing behavior.

The default configuration has 1,000 travelers. Route A has a free-flow travel time of 5 minutes and capacity of 500 vehicles per modeled interval, while Route B has a free-flow time of 15 minutes and capacity of 800. The travel-time function uses an exponent of two. Human learning uses a rate of 0.2, an exploration probability of 0.1, and a default preference-spread parameter of 5. The main comparison allows the human-only system to stabilize during days 1–100, measures its behavior during days 101–200, introduces the fleet at day 200, and evaluates the adapted mixed system during days 301–400. Fleet share, objectives, demand, and human preferences are varied to examine the sensitivity of the results.

The experiments show that autonomous coordination can benefit or disadvantage human drivers depending on the fleet’s objective and size. At modest fleet shares, self-interested strategies can improve fleet travel times while making conditions worse for the remaining human drivers. A socially oriented objective can improve overall efficiency, but its benefits need not be distributed equally. Larger fleet shares also change the effectiveness of strategies that disadvantage other users, and some settings produce oscillating outcomes. These findings demonstrate why aggregate travel-time savings should be examined alongside results for individual traveler groups.

The simplified network makes the interaction between objectives, preferences, and congestion easier to identify, but limits direct predictions for a complex city. The assumption that the operator perfectly anticipates human choices gives the fleet an informational advantage that may be difficult to achieve in practice. Static route-cost functions also omit queue spillback and detailed driving interactions. The contribution to this survey is therefore an explanation of possible social consequences of routing coordination. It complements RouteRL by showing why the objective assigned to automated travelers matters before a particular learning algorithm or large network is chosen.

Traffic Control and Coordination

Studies in this category examine how signal control, lane access, and corridor capacity influence traffic movement and congestion through interactions among vehicles, travelers, and infrastructure.

A Distributed Approach for Coordination of Traffic Signal Agents

Bazzan, A. L. C. 2005. A Distributed Approach for Coordination of Traffic Signal Agents. Autonomous Agents and Multi-Agent Systems 10, 2 (2005), 131–164.

View the abstract summary and brief comment

Bazzan investigates how traffic signals can coordinate through local decisions while responding to changing traffic conditions. Each intersection is represented by an agent responsible for selecting a signal plan. The central difficulty is that a plan benefiting one intersection may disrupt coordination along the road, while maintaining coordination may prevent that intersection from addressing its own congestion. The proposed approach uses evolutionary game theory and reinforcement learning to explore how compatible choices can emerge through repeated interactions. Agents adjust their behavior according to the rewards received from previous choices, allowing signal coordination to develop without requiring continuous negotiation between neighboring controllers. The traffic manager remains responsible for broader policy decisions while agents handle operational choices.

The main example is an arterial road with ten signalized intersections, with additional coordination experiments using 35 agents. Each intersection chooses between two previously designed signal plans that favor travel in opposite directions. When neighboring intersections select compatible plans, vehicles can progress along the road with fewer interruptions. Local detectors provide information about traffic conditions, and each agent updates the probability of selecting a plan using traffic changes and accumulated rewards. A memory parameter controls how strongly previous experience influences these updates. The experiments examine both rewards that encourage a shared corridor objective and rewards reflecting individual intersections’ conditions. Traffic movement is evaluated using a macroscopic simulator that represents flow through density ranges, rather than tracking the detailed behavior of individual drivers.

The model separates traffic movement, local observation, and signal-plan learning. Its interaction range is set to one, so agents coordinate with immediately neighboring intersections. Signal plans must already satisfy timing and safety constraints; learning selects among these plans rather than generating unrestricted signal timings. Three important settings govern adaptation: the frequency of learning, the frequency of local traffic changes, and the memory factor. The experiments identify learning-frequency settings of 5 and 10 as useful choices, corresponding to a learning probability of one-fifth or one-tenth per decision period. Tested memory factors include 0.80, 0.95, and 1.00. In this formulation, smaller values retain more influence from earlier payoffs and can slow adjustment. These settings need to be considered together because frequent learning can respond to insufficient information, while excessive reliance on previous conditions can delay coordination after demand changes.

The results show that the benefits of decentralized control depend on the traffic scenario. When demand is stable and clearly heavier in one direction, centralized signal synchronization performs better because there is little reason to interrupt that coordinated flow. When both directions place competing demands on the signals, the agent-based approach generally produces equal or lower traffic-density ranges on the evaluated lanes. Its flexibility allows intersections to temporarily depart from the coordinated plan when local conditions require attention. Under lighter traffic, the improvement is smaller. These findings support adaptive coordination in situations where local needs conflict, while also showing that it does not automatically improve on centralized control in every setting.

The learning experiments help explain these differences. Coordination becomes harder when traffic changes frequently, when local rewards conflict with the shared objective, or when agents give too much weight to older experience. Updating too frequently can also cause agents to learn an unsuitable pattern before gathering enough information. In a separate experiment, notifying neighbors about intended strategy changes generally slows coordination because those messages can trigger further changes nearby. This result concerns the particular communication rule tested; it does not show that communication is inherently harmful. The evaluation also has practical limits: it uses a simplified arterial, two available plans, and simulated traffic-density measures. It therefore provides evidence about coordination behavior and relative traffic performance, rather than establishing precise travel-time savings or effectiveness across a deployed citywide network.

For the survey, this paper shows how congestion management can be studied through the decisions of traffic infrastructure agents. Its main contribution is explaining the tension between serving an individual intersection and maintaining coordinated movement along a corridor. The findings suggest that adaptive signal control is most useful when changing local demands justify departing from a shared plan, provided the agents can learn quickly enough to respond. Together with Raney’s study, it also establishes an important distinction for our classification: congestion can be influenced through travelers adapting their routes or through controllers adapting how road capacity is allocated. For the future DEVSJAVA work, the intersection agents, detector inputs, and signal-plan changes offer a conceptual basis for interacting components, although the paper does not implement its approach in DEVSJAVA.

Analyzing communication policies in cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning for traffic signal control: A simulation-based study

Abidi, S. et al. 2025. Analyzing communication policies in cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning for traffic signal control: A simulation-based study. Simulation Modelling Practice and Theory 141, Article 103100 (May 2025). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.simpat.2025.103100 .

View the abstract summary and brief comment

Abidi and colleagues investigate what neighboring traffic-signal agents should communicate to coordinate their decisions effectively. Their Cooperative Deep Q-Network approach compares several information-sharing policies, including complete observations, pressure and signal phases, reward and phases, and reward and pressure. The central issue is that an intersection cannot understand surrounding congestion from local measurements alone, but receiving more information does not necessarily improve its decisions. The study therefore examines how the content of communication affects learning and traffic performance.

The system connects microscopic traffic movement in SUMO, a reinforcement-learning environment provided through SUMO-RL, and agent training through RLlib. Each signal agent observes its current phase, elapsed phase duration, incoming-lane densities and queues, and the information supplied by neighboring intersections. Intersections count as neighbors when they are directly connected by a lane. Agents choose signal-phase actions subject to timing restrictions: the minimum green time is 5 seconds, the maximum is 50 seconds, and a phase change includes a 3-second yellow interval. Local rewards measure the reduction in cumulative waiting time, while the cooperative formulation also incorporates shared experiences and a global reward based on the agents’ individual rewards.

The experiments use a 4 × 4 grid with 16 signalized intersections and 150-meter links containing two lanes. Demand alternates between balanced directional flows and a pattern with heavier west–east traffic. Each agent’s neural network has two hidden layers of 250 neurons with ReLU activation. Other reported settings include a discount factor of 0.99, a training batch size of 512, a replay buffer holding 10,000 experiences, and a learning start threshold of 1,000 collected experiences. These settings describe the learning process, while network geometry, vehicle arrival rates, and signal timings define the traffic environment. Keeping those categories separate makes it easier to determine whether performance changes originate in the controller or the scenario.

Sharing rewards and phases produces the lowest reported average delay among the compared communication policies in all three evaluated traffic scenarios. For example, the reported delay in the unbalanced-demand scenario is 195 seconds for reward-and-phase sharing, compared with 845 seconds for complete sharing. This supports the idea that concise information about neighboring actions and outcomes can be more useful than a larger observation containing less relevant detail. Results aggregated across five simulation runs provide evidence of performance variability, although the regular grid remains a limited representation of urban street networks.

This study connects congestion reduction to the design of agent communication rather than simply the choice of a learning algorithm. It also extends the coordination question raised by Bazzan: agents need information that helps them coordinate without making their responses unstable or unnecessarily complicated. However, performance on a simulated grid does not establish that the same communication policy will remain best on irregular networks or with unreliable measurements and communication delays. Applying the approach elsewhere would require testing the observation design, demand patterns, and learning settings together, rather than transferring the reported configuration unchanged.

Moving between these representations requires more than simply changing the level of detail: individual vehicle states must be converted into aggregate quantities, and aggregate traffic must be converted back into plausible vehicles. The authors use averaging and a probabilistic vehicle-generation method to perform these conversions. Model setup requires road geometry, initial traffic conditions, boundary flows, and a division of the network into agent-based and continuum regions. Important settings include the car-following and lane-changing parameters, continuum cell size, initial vehicle distribution, and criteria for choosing which regions receive detailed simulation. The simulator calculates a stable time step from the continuum dynamics and advances the agent-based regions using that same interval. Performance experiments include configurations with approximately 1% and 10% of vehicles simulated individually. Increasing the detailed portion raises computational cost but preserves more individual behavior. The configuration must therefore reflect the research question: a vehicle whose identity needs to remain traceable must stay within an agent-based region because aggregation removes that identity.

The performance experiments show substantial computational benefits on a large road network under several traffic densities. In the reported single-threaded benchmarks, the hybrid configurations run approximately 9–17 times faster than the fully agent-based simulation, while the entirely continuum-based model runs approximately 10–20 times faster. FLAME-GPU for Traffic Systems: A Scalable Agent-Based Simulation Framework

Smilovitskiy, M. et al. 2025. FLAME-GPU for Traffic Systems: A Scalable Agent-Based Simulation Framework. Systems 13, 5, Article 376 (May 2025). https://doi.org/10.3390/systems13050376 .

View the abstract summary and brief comment

Smilovitskiy and colleagues investigate how GPU parallelism can make large vehicle populations affordable to simulate. Their framework implements microscopic traffic behavior through FLAME-GPU and compares its performance with SUMO. Unlike approaches that gain speed by aggregating vehicles, this method retains individual vehicle agents and changes how their calculations are executed. The main contribution concerns simulation capacity and runtime rather than a particular congestion-control policy.

Vehicle agents maintain movement information such as position and speed and follow a modified Krauss car-following model. Lane-changing and junction rules determine how they interact with nearby traffic. Agents move through pre-insertion, driving, and pre-removal states, allowing safe entry, movement, and final data collection to be handled separately. Network-based messaging supplies information about vehicles on the same or adjacent road segments. Road-section agents support data collection, and traffic-light agents follow fixed signal patterns. GPU kernels execute agent functions in parallel while shared network data support upstream and downstream queries.

Setup begins with a road network and demand files prepared using SUMO tools. The Isle of Wight network is derived from OpenStreetMap, cleaned, and converted into SUMO’s network format. Random trips and precomputed routes supply vehicle demand, while vehicles choose lanes during simulation. Important behavioral inputs include desired speed, acceleration, braking, reaction time, gap acceptance, and random driving variation. Benchmark configurations vary insertion density from 60 to 600 vehicles per hour per kilometer of network length and include synthetic grids ranging from 5 × 5 to 100 × 100. The experiments run one-hour simulations three times on a workstation with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU.

The reported benchmarks show substantial speed advantages as vehicle populations grow. For approximately 95,000 vehicles in the Isle of Wight experiment, FLAME-GPU takes roughly 36 seconds compared with 2,481 seconds for SUMO, corresponding to the reported improvement of about 68 times. However, SUMO performs better at inserting vehicles into the network. Differences in how congestion clears can affect the space available for later vehicles, so loaded demand and vehicles actually inserted need to be considered alongside runtime. These results demonstrate computational potential without establishing identical traffic trajectories between the implementations.

The framework does not yet include all the capabilities available in SUMO, including adaptive traffic lights and vehicle teleportation. The authors also identify roundabout behavior and speed-limit transitions as areas needing improvement. Consequently, the speed results should not be interpreted as proof of equivalent behavioral realism or readiness for every traffic-control application. For congestion research, the approach offers more room to test large populations and multiple demand conditions, provided the implemented driving and control rules are adequate for the study. Together with Sewall’s hybrid method, it illustrates two different ways to manage simulation cost: distributing individual calculations or changing the level of traffic detail.

A New Discrete Event Simulation of Large-Scale Car Traffic at Microscopic Level

Kieu, T. P. et al. 2025. A New Discrete Event Simulation of Large-Scale Car Traffic at Microscopic Level. In Proceedings of the 39th ACM SIGSIM Conference on Principles of Advanced Discrete Simulation (SIGSIM-PADS ’25), 143–153. https://doi.org/10.1145/3726301.3728409 .

View the abstract summary and brief comment

Kieu and colleagues examine whether microscopic traffic simulation can run more efficiently by updating vehicle movement at meaningful events instead of at every fixed time step. Their DEv-CF model concentrates on events such as a vehicle entering a road, catching up with its leader, beginning to move, or leaving the road. Between events, positions can be advanced using the current movement assumptions. This reduces repeated calculations, but it also simplifies how acceleration and deceleration are represented. The main contribution is therefore a way to balance computational cost against the detail of individual vehicle interactions.

The implemented system connects three DEVS atomic models: Generator, Road, and Transducer. The Generator introduces vehicles, the Road manages their movement and interactions, and the Transducer collects results. Vehicles are objects processed within these components rather than separate atomic models. Within the Road component, one queue holds moving vehicles and another holds vehicles waiting to enter movement. Each vehicle carries information such as its position, speed, length, and time until its next relevant event. The Road component schedules its next transition from these event times and recalculates them when a new arrival or interaction changes the situation.

Setting up the model requires road length and speed limit, vehicle lengths and desired speeds, a minimum following gap, and vehicle departure times. The authors generate matching demand inputs for fwkDEVS and SUMO, with desired speeds between 8 and 13 meters per second. Their small experiment uses 10 vehicles on a one-kilometer road, while the medium experiment uses 10,000 vehicles over 24 hours on a 20-kilometer road. Larger experiments increase total demand to as many as one million vehicles on that road. These totals describe vehicles introduced over the scenario, not necessarily vehicles present simultaneously. The demand profile assigns more departures to morning and afternoon peak periods.

Evaluation compares DEv-CF with SUMO’s Intelligent Driver Model under the same demand inputs. Vehicle trajectories broadly agree in the small experiments, although the simplified speed changes produce local differences. In the medium experiment, road occupancy follows similar daily patterns, but DEv-CF accommodates somewhat more vehicles during peak periods because the models differ in vehicle spacing. Across the large experiments, the authors report that fwkDEVS runs about 2.89 times faster on average. It also produces fewer output records because it records relevant events rather than every time step. The comparison changes both the movement model and the simulation implementation, so the speed advantage cannot be attributed to event scheduling alone.

This paper gives the survey a concrete example of how simulation design affects the cost of studying congestion. Faster experiments could make repeated demand or control comparisons more practical, provided that the model retains the interactions important to the question being studied. However, the reported tests concern a single-road setting and comparison with another simulator, not independent validation against observed traffic or a demonstrated congestion-reduction policy. Intersections, lane changes, and network routing would require further development and testing. Its Generator–Road–Transducer organization is also a useful conceptual reference for DEVSJAVA, although the implementation reported here uses fwkDEVS.

Autonomic Partition-Aware Malleable Microscopic Traffic Simulation

Siguenza-Torres, A. et al. 2025. Autonomic Partition-Aware Malleable Microscopic Traffic Simulation. In Proceedings of the 39th ACM SIGSIM Conference on Principles of Advanced Discrete Simulation (SIGSIM-PADS ’25), 132–142. https://doi.org/10.1145/3726301.3728412 .

View the abstract summary and brief comment

Siguenza-Torres and colleagues address the changing computing demands of large microscopic traffic simulations. A fixed allocation of processors can waste resources during quiet periods yet struggle when traffic becomes heavier or moves differently across the network. Their extension, Autonomic CityMoS, changes the number of computing processes during a run. The goal is to maintain a chosen simulation pace with fewer resources, rather than simply maximize execution speed. Here, simulation pace means simulated time divided by elapsed real time, so a target of four represents running four times faster than real time.

The architecture separates the traffic simulator from an external resource manager. Profilers inside CityMoS collect workload and performance measurements, while the manager uses a policy to decide whether the allocation should change. Roads form the vertices of a partitioning graph, and road connections form its edges. Accumulated vehicle counts describe work on each road, while movements between roads help estimate communication requirements. METIS partitions this graph, and smoothed observations support predictions for the next decision period. When a change is requested, CityMoS saves its state, stops, and restarts from that snapshot with the new allocation.

Implementation requires a road network and demand scenario, workload profilers, a decision interval, available process counts, and a target pace. The authors compare a simple cost model based on average vehicles per process and process count with a partition-aware model based on the busiest partition and vehicle migrations between partitions. Coefficients are fitted from observed runtime data. The policy searches for the smallest allocation predicted to meet its target, or selects the best predicted pace if none can meet it. Experiments use up to 16 MPI ranks across eight machines, two 90-by-90 intersection grids, and an irregular network. One grid experiment changes traffic direction patterns after 15 simulated hours to test adaptation to a changing workload.

The results show that resource adaptation is useful under some conditions but is not automatically cheaper. In the unidirectional grid experiment, snapshot and restart overhead make total resource use higher than the static comparison. Savings appear in the bidirectional and irregular-network experiments. In the irregular network, the simple model uses 56.1 percent of the static run’s CPU time and the partition-aware model uses 72.6 percent, including adaptation overhead. However, the simpler model also falls further below the target pace. These results make the trade-off clear: using fewer resources is not equivalent to maintaining the required responsiveness. Accounting for partition characteristics improves decisions when vehicle migrations and workload balance change.

The contribution to congestion research is computational rather than a traffic-control policy. Large experiments need to remain practical when queues and vehicle activity are unevenly distributed, especially if simulation is intended to support interactive analysis. The study shows why vehicle count alone may be an inadequate predictor of computing cost. Its main limitation is that the adaptation policy does not include snapshot and restart costs in its decisions, even though these costs are included in the resource-use evaluation. The experiments also assess execution performance rather than agreement with observed traffic. Extending this approach would require considering adaptation overhead alongside prediction accuracy and the deadlines of the intended application.

Calibration and Validation Methods

Studies in this category examine how simulation inputs are estimated, how model outputs are compared with observations, and what evidence supports the credibility of traffic predictions.

Overcoming Data Scarcity in Calibrating SUMO Scenarios With Evolutionary Algorithms

Kappenberger, J. and Stuckenschmidt, H. 2025. Overcoming Data Scarcity in Calibrating SUMO Scenarios With Evolutionary Algorithms. In SUMO Conference Proceedings 6 (Jul. 2025), 133–148. https://doi.org/10.52825/scp.v6i.2590 .

View the abstract summary and brief comment

Kappenberger and Stuckenschmidt address a practical difficulty in building credible traffic simulations: detailed demand information is often unavailable even when some traffic counts exist. Their Mannheim scenario combines GPS-derived routes with induction-loop and camera observations, then adjusts route probabilities through evolutionary optimization. The objective is to generate demand that produces the observed counts after traffic is actually simulated. This matters because a route set that appears compatible with counts may still create bottlenecks that prevent the expected vehicles from reaching the sensors.

The method connects data preparation, route sampling, microscopic simulation, and optimization. GPS traces are matched to the road network using a Hidden Markov Model, and disconnected or implausible routes are checked before calibration. The processed data provide 6,617 unique routes and their observed frequencies. SUMO’s routeSampler uses these routes and traffic counts to construct demand. A simulation then measures the difference between generated and observed induction-loop counts. CMA-ES adjusts weights applied to the initial route frequencies and repeats this process, learning which demand distributions better reproduce the observations.

The experiments cover a morning period from 6 to 9 a.m. and a full 24-hour day. The three-hour optimization uses 100 generations with 120 candidate solutions per generation. Its result initializes the full-day optimization, which uses 50 generations with the same population size. These candidates are alternative route-weight configurations, not individual vehicle agents. The initial weights are all one, so the search starts from the GPS-derived frequencies. In a separate final full-day experiment, the authors additionally set rerouting probability to 0.2, simulation step length to 0.25 seconds, and maximum departure delay to 100 seconds. Vehicles unable to enter within that delay are discarded, which is an important demand-handling assumption.

The results show improvements at several stages. For the three-hour scenario, weighting the GPS-derived routes produces an absolute relative error of 0.19, which falls to 0.04 after optimization. The optimized full-day scenario reaches 0.14, and the final rerouting and simulation-setting variant reduces it to 0.07. The last improvement cannot be attributed to rerouting alone because several settings change together. Computational cost is also substantial: the reported optimization takes approximately 15 hours for the shorter scenario and 148 hours for the full-day scenario, using parallel execution.

Close agreement with counts does not uniquely identify the routes that real travelers used. The study calibrates and evaluates against observations from one date, while the commercial GPS data come from another period and may not represent all travelers. The authors therefore describe a plausible demand configuration rather than a reconstruction proven correct throughout the network. Its contribution to congestion research is the connection between input quality and trustworthy policy evaluation: unrealistic demand can create misleading queues or apparent intervention benefits. Testing other dates and independent observations would strengthen confidence before applying the calibrated scenario to new policies.

Advancing the MATSim Open Berlin Scenario: Improvements in transport scenario generation and calibration methods

Rakow, C. et al. 2025. Advancing the MATSim Open Berlin Scenario: Improvements in transport scenario generation and calibration methods. Transportation Research Procedia 86 (2025), 732–739. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trpro.2025.04.091 .

View the abstract summary and brief comment

Rakow and colleagues improve the Open Berlin MATSim scenario by updating its demand-generation methods and automating parts of its calibration. Their starting point is that a useful traffic model needs credible activity patterns, destinations, mode choices, and road conditions before it can evaluate a transport policy. Earlier versions relied on activity-generation assumptions that were not specifically developed for Berlin and had become outdated. The revised workflow uses more detailed local information and checks several different outputs, making the paper especially useful for understanding how the quality of model inputs affects congestion analysis.

The scenario is assembled through connected stages for network construction, population synthesis, activity scheduling, location choice, and mode calibration. OpenStreetMap supplies road and facility information, while GTFS supplies public transport schedules. Microscopic simulation helps estimate intersection capacities, and routing information helps account for delays within MATSim’s link speeds. Synthetic people receive travel diaries by statistical matching with survey respondents of similar demographic characteristics. Activity locations are then assigned using facility types and reported travel distances. Each agent receives five plausible location plans, with plan selection calibrated against traffic counts.

A central configuration step concerns the alternative-specific constants used in mode-choice utility. These constants represent influences not explicitly captured by travel time and monetary cost. After a simulation run, the procedure compares simulated mode shares with survey shares and adjusts the constants using their logarithmic ratios. One mode must remain fixed as a reference; walking has a constant of zero in the reported setup. The evaluated release uses a 10 percent population sample. Reported monetary settings include 0.149 euros per kilometer for car travel, five euros per day for driving, and three euros per day for public transport. These are scenario assumptions, while the resulting mode constants are calibrated outputs rather than values to transfer unchanged to another city.

The revised scenario closely reproduces the survey’s mode shares, with a reported mean absolute error below 0.5 percentage points, and improves the representation of trip-distance distributions. Evaluation also considers travel-time estimates for 1,000 sampled routes from online routing services and observed daily road counts. The model captures peak-period speed reductions, but produces a midday speed increase that is less apparent in the reference estimates. Road volumes remain too low, especially on motorways. The authors connect these discrepancies to incomplete commercial traffic, through traffic from outside the modeled region, and tourist activity.

The study shows why calibration should be judged across several measures. A close match to mode shares does not establish that road demand, queues, and travel times are equally accurate, particularly when some of the comparison data also guide calibration. Parking search is not explicitly modeled, and mode-choice constants can compensate for omitted influences without reproducing their physical effects. Several data sources also describe different years. For congestion research, the contribution is a more reproducible and better-supported baseline for later policy experiments, rather than evidence that a particular intervention reduces congestion. Its staged workflow provides a practical example of how demand, network characteristics, and behavioral calibration must be considered together.

Verification and Calibration of Microscopic Traffic Simulation Using Driver Behavior and Car-Following Metrics for Freeway Segments

Medina, J. C. et al. 2024. Verification and Calibration of Microscopic Traffic Simulation Using Driver Behavior and Car-Following Metrics for Freeway Segments. Technical Report FHWA-HRT-24-103. Federal Highway Administration. https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/74233 .

View the abstract summary and brief comment

Medina and colleagues address a weakness in traffic simulation calibration: matching overall travel times, delays, or queues does not necessarily mean that individual vehicles behave realistically. Their report uses naturalistic driving observations to establish reference distributions for car-following behavior. The data include more than 1,600 hours of following behavior from more than 1,700 drivers across 104 urban and suburban freeway sites in North Carolina, Washington, and Florida. The resulting targets describe vehicle spacing, acceleration, and changes in acceleration, allowing simulated interactions to be examined alongside aggregate traffic performance.

The workflow connects trajectory preparation, leader-follower identification, traffic-condition grouping, statistical comparison, and parameter revision. Observed data are cleaned and aligned before sustained following episodes are extracted. The authors use overlapping speed ranges to distinguish operating conditions while retaining episodes whose speeds vary within a reasonable range. Their NACT tool applies a comparable analysis to generic trajectories exported by a microscopic simulator. Vehicle spacing becomes the main calibration target because its distributions distinguish speed groups more clearly than the other examined measures. The tool evaluates model outputs without requiring a particular car-following formulation.

Important setup choices concern data quality and comparison rules rather than neural-network layers. In preparing the naturalistic data, the researchers interpolate missing observations only across gaps of at most two seconds and require at least 10 consecutive seconds of following behavior within a selected speed group. Ten overlapping groups cover speeds from approximately 5 to 85 miles per hour, with standstill spacing considered separately. NACT compares distributions using Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Cramér–von Mises tests and displays 95 percent confidence bands. The proposed sequence is to calibrate aggregate traffic measures, assess microscopic behavior, revise relevant parameters, and then recheck the aggregate measures because the adjustments can affect both levels.

The empirical analysis provides behavior targets, while a separate exploratory exercise examines consequences for simulated safety measures. That exercise connects VISSIM trajectories to the Surrogate Safety Assessment Model and compares default and calibrated settings at 12 freeway sites. The researchers process 10 runs for each parameter condition and extract two hours of usable simulation data per run. Calibrated scenarios show modest improvements in the relationship between simulated conflicts and observed crashes, rather than a large or universal improvement. Additional crash models associate greater variability in speed and density, and smaller mean spacing, with higher crash frequencies; these associations do not establish causation.

This report strengthens the survey’s treatment of validation by showing how apparently favorable congestion results can depend on unrealistic interactions between vehicles. It offers a way to check those interactions before relying on predicted benefits from a new policy. However, NACT does not automatically choose parameters or explain which parameter to change; that task remains with the modeler. Its reference data concern passenger vehicles on freeways in daytime and good weather, so direct use for urban intersections or other conditions requires justification. The safety experiments also leave heavily congested conditions insufficiently explored. The contribution is therefore a stronger calibration and checking process, not a demonstrated congestion-reduction intervention or a guarantee of predictive accuracy.

Cross-Paper Comparison and Findings

The following comparisons connect the findings across the 20 reviewed papers. They distinguish evidence about congestion interventions from contributions to behavioral modeling, computational efficiency, and simulation credibility.

Travel decisions connect demand with congestion

Charypar, Raney, Akman, and Jamróz examine different parts of the relationship between individual decisions and traffic conditions. Charypar explains how activities, locations, and schedules generate trips, while Raney connects route selection to congestion experienced during previous journeys. Akman and Jamróz extend the route-choice problem to interactions between human travelers and coordinated autonomous vehicles. Together, these studies show why demand cannot always be treated as a fixed input: the decisions producing it may change with travel conditions and the objectives assigned to agents.

However, the studies represent different levels of behavioral detail. Charypar’s experiments do not include congestion feedback, while Akman’s illustrative travelers repeat individual origin–destination trips rather than complete daily schedules. Jamróz uses a two-route system to isolate conflicts between fleet and human interests. Their contributions are complementary, but no single study establishes a complete, empirically validated account of how daily activities, route learning, and automated coordination interact across a city.

Control depends on information and operating conditions

Bazzan, Abidi, and Liu show that adaptive signal control involves more than choosing a learning algorithm. Bazzan emphasizes the tension between local intersection needs and coordinated corridor movement. Abidi examines which information neighboring controllers should exchange, while Liu investigates how traffic observations are represented before an action is selected. Observations, rewards, timing constraints, and adaptation rates therefore form important parts of the model.

Greater complexity does not automatically improve performance. Bazzan identifies conditions in which centralized synchronization performs better, and Abidi’s experiments favor a particular concise information-sharing policy over complete sharing. Pradhananga offers a different approach by repeatedly simulating candidate signal settings using camera-derived observations. This creates a comparison between learning a control policy and evaluating alternatives during operation. Neither approach is universally preferable; their usefulness depends on demand changes, observation quality, available computing time, and the extent of coordination between intersections.

Benefits can be uneven or shift congestion elsewhere

Akman and Jamróz show that coordinated vehicles can improve their own travel times while worsening conditions for human drivers. Alnajjar demonstrates a related spatial issue: increased corridor capacity produces uneven effects on surrounding roads. Dubey’s FAIRLANE results further show that shared priority-lane benefits depend on vehicle composition. A policy that uses spare capacity effectively at lower autonomous-vehicle shares may become less advantageous as those shares increase.

Chen and Pradhananga also illustrate why the performance measure matters. Chen balances traveler costs and revenue, so the preferred policy does not necessarily maximize speed. In Pradhananga’s experiments, higher throughput can accompany longer average travel time. Congestion policies should therefore be evaluated through several measures, with attention to who benefits, where changes occur, and whether delays are transferred elsewhere. A favorable network average can conceal these differences.

Faster methods preserve different information

The computational studies address different sources of simulation cost. Sewall changes the level of detail across the network, Khan evaluates simpler alternatives to behavioral simulation, and Natterer learns an approximation of simulation outputs. Smilovitskiy retains individual vehicles while using GPU parallelism. Kieu updates movement through discrete events, while Siguenza-Torres adjusts the computing resources assigned to a running simulation.

These methods should be compared according to the information needed for the research question. Sewall’s aggregation can lose vehicle identity, Khan’s static routing omits congestion-dependent behavioral feedback, and Natterer’s daily-volume predictions do not directly describe queues or individual trajectories. Parallel execution does not by itself establish equivalent traffic behavior, and Kieu’s results depend on both event scheduling and a simplified movement model. Faster execution is valuable when it enables additional scenarios or repeated runs, but it is not evidence that a congestion policy is more effective.

Credibility requires more than one matching outcome

Kappenberger, Rakow, and Medina examine credibility at different levels. Kappenberger adjusts route probabilities to reproduce traffic counts. Rakow connects population, activity, location, mode-choice, and network preparation, examining several resulting travel measures. Medina compares simulated following behavior with naturalistic observations. Together, these studies show that demand, travel choices, and vehicle interactions require different forms of evidence.

Agreement with one measure does not establish overall realism. Different demand patterns can reproduce similar counts, accurate mode shares can coexist with underestimated road volumes, and acceptable aggregate outcomes can conceal unrealistic vehicle spacing. Raney also raises the question of whether evaluation observations helped prepare the model’s demand. Calibration fit, independent observational evaluation, and agreement with another simulator should therefore be distinguished. Khan, Kieu, and Natterer provide useful model-to-model evidence, but that answers a different question from validation against observed traffic.

Model setup should follow the mechanism being studied

Across the papers, system design can be understood through the transportation environment, agent decisions, interactions, and evaluation process. These are analytical distinctions rather than a requirement for four literal software layers. The environment defines roads, demand, and capacity. Decision mechanisms select activities, routes, signal actions, or lane access. Interactions connect these decisions through vehicle movement, communication, and feedback. Evaluation measures traffic outcomes, their credibility, and the computational cost of producing them.

This distinction explains why parameters cannot be transferred unchanged between studies. A route-learning rate, signal reward, following gap, and neural-network learning rate control different mechanisms. Chen’s policy weights express a balance between objectives, Rakow’s calibrated mode constants help match observed behavior, and Siguenza-Torres’s target pace specifies a computing requirement. Comparisons should identify what each setting controls, how its value was chosen, and whether conclusions remain stable when important assumptions change.

Implications for congestion research and future modeling

The reviewed papers suggest that a credible congestion study needs a connected sequence of demand preparation, behavioral modeling, intervention design, simulation, and evaluation. An effective controller can produce misleading results if demand is unrealistic, while a well-calibrated baseline does not establish that a new intervention will work. Computational improvements make broader experimentation possible, but those experiments still require suitable assumptions and supporting evidence.

Further work should test interventions across multiple demand conditions, agent objectives, and plausible parameter settings, examining both aggregate outcomes and effects on particular travelers or locations. Clearer configuration reporting and stronger separation between calibration and independent evaluation would make results easier to assess and reproduce.

For a future DEVSJAVA implementation, these findings support separating demand generation, road movement, traveler decisions, controllers, and measurement components, with explicit feedback between them. This provides an organized basis for experimentation without assuming that modular implementation alone guarantees realistic traffic.
