Shortlist: 4 foundational papers

1. An Agent-Based Microsimulation Model of Swiss Travel: First Results

Raney, B. et al. 2003. An Agent-Based Microsimulation Model of Swiss Travel: First Results. Networks and Spatial Economics 3, 1 (Jan. 2003), 23–41. https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022096916806 .

PDF version provided.

Abstract Summary: This paper presents a modular transportation simulation that represents individual travelers and allows their route choices to change through repeated simulation and learning. The implementation combines travel demand, route planning, traffic simulation, and feedback, using experienced travel times to improve agents’ plans over successive iterations. Experiments model approximately one million car trips during the Swiss morning peak and compare simulated traffic volumes with observed counts and an existing transportation model. The results demonstrate the feasibility of large regional agent-based simulations and show how repeated route adjustment can redistribute traffic across the network.

Comments: Raney and colleagues combine individual route planning, a queue-based traffic simulation, and repeated feedback to model how travelers adapt to congestion across Switzerland. Their experiments show that learning from previous trips spreads traffic across alternative routes, while comparisons with traffic counts demonstrate the importance of checking simulated patterns against observations. This provides a foundation for our survey because a congestion intervention can change travelers’ decisions, which then change the traffic conditions the intervention was intended to improve. However, the tested model uses car trips derived from origin–destination matrices and adjusts routes between simulation runs, limiting its representation of multimodal travel and rerouting during a journey.

2. A Distributed Approach for Coordination of Traffic Signal Agents

Bazzan, A. L. C. 2005. A Distributed Approach for Coordination of Traffic Signal Agents. Autonomous Agents and Multi-Agent Systems 10, 1 (Jan. 2005), 131–164. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10458-004-6975-9 .

Download & Read PDF version

Abstract Summary: This paper investigates how traffic signals can coordinate their decisions through a distributed mechanism based on evolutionary game theory. Signal agents adapt their strategies in response to traffic conditions and interactions with other agents, seeking coordination without relying entirely on a central controller. Simulation experiments compare the proposed mechanism with centralized signal synchronization under different patterns of directional demand. The distributed approach performs better when competing traffic streams require adaptation, while centralized synchronization remains effective when traffic consistently favors one direction.

Comments: Bazzan models traffic signals as autonomous agents that use evolutionary game theory to coordinate their timing while responding to local traffic conditions. In the simulated arterial, this adaptive approach performs better than centralized synchronization when both directions compete for priority, but centralized control performs better when demand consistently favors one direction. The study gives our survey an early example of how cooperation among signal agents can relieve congestion without relying entirely on a central controller. Its results also show why decentralized control should be evaluated under different demand patterns, since rapid changes can slow coordination and reduce its benefits.

3. Generating complete all-day activity plans with genetic algorithms

Charypar, D. and Nagel, K. 2005. Generating complete all-day activity plans with genetic algorithms. Transportation 32, 4 (Jul. 2005), 369–397. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11116-004-8287-y .

PDF version provided

Abstract Summary: This paper develops a genetic algorithm for generating complete daily activity plans for individual travelers. Candidate plans specify activities, locations, and timing, and are evaluated using a utility function that balances activity benefits against travel and scheduling costs. Selection, crossover, and mutation progressively improve these plans, with experiments examining both busy and less constrained daily schedules. The algorithm produces plausible plans with high utility in the tested examples, providing a method for generating individual travel demand, although the experiments use simplified travel-time estimates rather than feedback from a dynamic traffic simulation.

Comments: Charypar and Nagel use a genetic algorithm to construct daily activity plans that balance the benefits of activities against travel time and scheduling constraints. The algorithm produces plausible plans in the test scenarios by jointly choosing activities, locations, and time allocations, connecting individual preferences to the demand placed on a transport network. This is relevant to our survey because evaluating congestion policies requires understanding why people travel and how changes in travel conditions could alter their schedules. However, the experiments use simplified travel-time estimates, so congestion avoidance through feedback from a traffic simulation remains a proposed extension rather than a demonstrated result.

4. Interactive hybrid simulation of large-scale traffic

Sewall, J. et al. 2011. Interactive hybrid simulation of large-scale traffic. ACM Transactions on Graphics 30, 6, Article 135 (Dec. 2011), 12 pages. https://doi.org/10.1145/2070781.2024169 .

Open-access full text and PDF.

Abstract Summary: This paper introduces a hybrid traffic simulator that combines individual vehicle agents with a continuum model describing aggregate traffic flow. Detailed vehicle simulation operates in selected regions, while the aggregate model handles the rest of the network, with conversion procedures connecting the two representations. Experiments on real and synthetic road networks evaluate computational performance, and comparisons with recorded highway traffic examine the reproduction of observed flow patterns. The approach supports interactive simulation of large networks at lower computational cost than a fully agent-based model while retaining individual vehicle detail where it is needed.

Comments: Sewall and colleagues combine individual vehicle simulation with an aggregate traffic-flow model, allowing different parts of a road network to use different levels of detail. Their hybrid method runs faster than a fully agent-based simulation in the reported benchmarks and retains important traffic-flow patterns when compared with NGSIM freeway observations. For our survey, it offers a way to study local bottlenecks while keeping the surrounding network computationally manageable. Its main limitation is that switching between representations preserves traffic distributions more reliably than individual vehicle positions, which matters when interpreting detailed interactions near the boundaries.

5. Analyzing communication policies in cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning for traffic signal control: A simulation-based study

Abidi, S. et al. 2025. Analyzing communication policies in cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning for traffic signal control: A simulation-based study. Simulation Modelling Practice and Theory 141, Article 103100 (May 2025). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.simpat.2025.103100 .

Full text may require institutional access.

Abstract Summary: This study examines how information exchanged between traffic signal agents affects cooperative deep reinforcement learning under partial observation. Using a cooperative deep Q-network approach, the authors compare policies that share rewards, signal phases, traffic pressure, or more extensive neighboring information. Simulation experiments evaluate these policies under balanced, unbalanced, and mixed traffic conditions using traffic delay and learning performance. Sharing rewards and signal phases produces the strongest results across the tested scenarios, indicating that carefully chosen information can support coordination more effectively than sharing a larger amount of data.

Comments: Abidi and colleagues investigate how the information exchanged between learning-based signal controllers affects their ability to coordinate traffic. In their Co-DQN experiments, sharing rewards and signal phases produces lower delays than the other tested communication policies, including sharing all neighboring observations. This directly supports our survey’s congestion focus by showing that effective coordination depends on selecting useful information, rather than simply increasing the amount shared. The findings provide a concrete comparison with earlier distributed-control approaches, although their effectiveness beyond the tested simulation scenarios still requires validation.

6. RouteRL: Multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for urban route choice with autonomous vehicles

Akman, A. O. et al. 2025. RouteRL: Multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for urban route choice with autonomous vehicles. SoftwareX 31, Article 102279 (Sep. 2025). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.softx.2025.102279 .

Open-access full text and PDF.

Abstract Summary: RouteRL is an open-source framework for studying route choice in traffic networks shared by human drivers and autonomous vehicles. It combines SUMO traffic simulation with behavioral learning for human travelers and multi-agent reinforcement learning for autonomous vehicles, whose objectives can be configured to represent different routing strategies. Example applications in Cologne, Ingolstadt, and Manhattan examine how repeated route choices and the proportion of autonomous vehicles affect travel times. Initial results show that autonomous vehicles can improve their own journeys while increasing travel times for human drivers, illustrating how routing objectives influence both individual outcomes and network performance.

Comments: RouteRL combines behavioral models of human route choice with reinforcement-learning agents for autonomous vehicles in SUMO. Its experiments, including a Manhattan network, show how routing objectives and the proportion of autonomous vehicles can change travel times and the distribution of benefits between driver groups. This makes the framework useful for our survey because it allows congestion strategies to be assessed in terms of both overall efficiency and their effects on travelers outside an automated fleet. However, each agent repeats a single origin–destination trip and chooses its route before departure, leaving trip chains and rerouting during travel outside the current model.

7. Enhancing Travel Reservation Benefits Through Incentive and Penalty Mechanisms in Urban Congested Roads

Chen, H. et al. 2025. Enhancing Travel Reservation Benefits Through Incentive and Penalty Mechanisms in Urban Congested Roads. Applied Sciences 15, 3, Article 1393 (Jan. 2025). https://doi.org/10.3390/app15031393 .

Open-access full text and PDF.

Abstract Summary: This study examines whether incentives and penalties can improve participation and traffic outcomes in a travel reservation system. An agent-based model represents travelers with different values of time and travel choices, while a multiobjective optimization approach considers the interests of both travelers and system managers. Experiments on an extended Sioux Falls network evaluate changes in reservation participation, travel costs, revenue, and traffic conditions. The proposed mechanisms increase participation and revenue while reducing travel costs and improving average traffic speed in the tested scenarios, showing how traveler responses influence the effectiveness of reservation policies.

Comments: Chen and colleagues model a travel reservation system in which incentives and penalties encourage travelers to adjust departure times and transport modes. Their Sioux Falls experiments represent differences in travelers’ values of time and report improved traffic performance and lower travel costs when these mechanisms are introduced. The paper broadens our survey by examining how congestion can be reduced through changes in demand before vehicles enter crowded roads. Because the evidence comes from simulated choices, the reported benefits depend on whether actual travelers would participate and respond to incentives as the model assumes.

8. FAIRLANE: A multi-agent approach to priority lane management in diverse traffic composition

Dubey, R. K. et al. 2025. FAIRLANE: A multi-agent approach to priority lane management in diverse traffic composition. Transportation Research Part C: Emerging Technologies 171, Article 104919 (Feb. 2025). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trc.2024.104919 .

Open-access full text and PDF.

Abstract Summary: This paper investigates how priority lanes can be shared effectively among connected autonomous vehicles, semi-autonomous vehicles, and human-driven vehicles. The authors propose FAIRLANE, a multi-agent reinforcement learning framework that adapts lane-access decisions while considering traffic efficiency, driving comfort, and safety-related measures. Simulation experiments compare its performance with alternative lane-management strategies across different vehicle compositions. The results show reduced waiting times and increased average speeds in several tested settings, particularly when connected autonomous vehicles make up less than 40% of traffic and semi-autonomous vehicles account for half of the remaining vehicles.

Comments: The paper’s main contribution to our survey is its treatment of road-space allocation as an interaction among travelers with different vehicle capabilities. It supports the investigation of congestion reduction by examining whether more flexible access to existing lanes can make better use of available capacity during the transition to automated traffic. The dependence of its results on vehicle composition shows why a lane policy should be evaluated under several adoption scenarios before being recommended for a city. Its safety conclusions are more limited because they rely on approximate measures within SUMO rather than observed crash outcomes.

9. Social implications of coexistence of CAVs and human drivers in the context of route choice

Jamróz, G. et al. 2025. Social implications of coexistence of CAVs and human drivers in the context of route choice. Scientific Reports 15, Article 6768 (Feb. 2025). https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-90783-w .

Open-access full text and PDF.

Abstract Summary: This paper studies how coordinated route choices by connected and autonomous vehicles affect human drivers and overall traffic performance. A model with two alternative routes combines human learning from previous trips with autonomous fleet decisions guided by objectives ranging from fleet benefit to broader social benefit. Experiments vary traffic demand, fleet size, and human route preferences to examine both travel times and the distribution of benefits. The results show that coordination can improve or worsen outcomes for human drivers depending on the fleet’s objective and size, so lower total travel time does not necessarily imply that all travelers benefit equally.

Comments: Jamróz and colleagues use a two-route agent-based model to examine how coordinated autonomous vehicles interact with human drivers who learn from past journeys. Their results show that fleet objectives, market share, and human preferences can determine whether coordination improves overall travel times or benefits the fleet at other drivers’ expense. This is important for our survey because a congestion strategy should be judged by its effects across the network, including who gains and who experiences additional delay. The simplified network and analytical travel-time functions make these mechanisms easier to identify, but limit direct predictions for a complex city such as New York.

10. Simplifying traffic simulation - from Euclidean distances to agent-based models

Khan, T. S. et al. 2024. Simplifying traffic simulation - from Euclidean distances to agent-based models. Computational Urban Science 4, Article 32 (Nov. 2024). https://doi.org/10.1007/s43762-024-00145-x .

Open-access full text and PDF.

Abstract Summary: This paper compares simplified routing calculations with agent-based traffic simulation to examine the trade-off between computational cost and model detail. Using more than three million car trips in the Washington region, the authors compare GraphHopper routing with MATSim in terms of travel times, distances, route overlap, traffic volumes, and resource requirements. The simpler approach reproduces several broad travel and network patterns while requiring substantially less computation. However, its limited representation of congestion feedback means that agreement in aggregate measures does not establish equivalence for applications requiring interactions between traffic conditions and traveler decisions.

Comments: Khan and colleagues compare a simple GraphHopper routing approach with MATSim to determine how much traffic information can be reproduced with less computational effort. For more than three million car trips, GraphHopper produces broadly similar aggregate travel measures and traffic patterns, although differences remain in individual routes and congested areas. The study helps our survey assess when a fast approximation may be useful for preliminary analysis and when interactions among travelers justify a more detailed agent-based model. Since the tested routing approach does not dynamically account for congestion, similarity in average results alone does not establish that it can predict the effects of congestion interventions.

11. Machine learning surrogates for agent-based models in transportation policy analysis

Natterer, E. S. et al. 2025. Machine learning surrogates for agent-based models in transportation policy analysis. Transportation Research Part C: Emerging Technologies 180, Article 105360 (Nov. 2025). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trc.2025.105360 .

Open-access full text and PDF.

Abstract Summary: This paper develops a machine learning surrogate that approximates the output of an agent-based transportation model for faster policy analysis. The model combines graph-based learning with Transformer attention to predict changes in daily traffic volumes on individual road segments following road-capacity interventions. Training and evaluation use a large collection of MATSim simulations for the Paris road network, covering more than 30,000 segments. The surrogate predicts traffic-volume changes accurately and much faster than running the full simulation, although producing the training data requires substantial computation and application to new settings would require further validation.

Comments: Natterer and colleagues train machine-learning surrogates on Paris MATSim simulations to predict how road-capacity reductions redistribute traffic across street segments. Their best model reproduces simulated daily traffic-volume changes with an R² of 0.91 while evaluating scenarios far faster than the underlying simulation. This supports our survey by making it more practical to screen many policy options before examining promising congestion interventions in detail. However, daily volumes can conceal peak-hour delays, and the surrogate inherits the source simulation’s limitations while requiring new training data for different cities or policy types.

12. FLAME-GPU for Traffic Systems: A Scalable Agent-Based Simulation Framework

Smilovitskiy, M. et al. 2025. FLAME-GPU for Traffic Systems: A Scalable Agent-Based Simulation Framework. Systems 13, 5, Article 376 (May 2025). https://doi.org/10.3390/systems13050376 .

Open-access full text and PDF.

Abstract Summary: This paper presents a GPU-based framework for simulating large populations of individual vehicles using FLAME GPU. Vehicle agents follow a modified Krauss car-following model, and parallel execution handles vehicle updates and interactions across the road network. Benchmarks on an Isle of Wight network and two synthetic grid networks compare the framework with CPU-based SUMO in terms of runtime and vehicle-processing performance. The framework achieves substantial speed improvements in the tested cases, while differences in vehicle insertion and supported traffic-management features qualify the comparison between the two simulators.

Comments: Smilovitskiy and colleagues use GPU parallelism to accelerate simulations of individual vehicles on the Isle of Wight road network and synthetic grids. Their benchmarks show substantial runtime improvements over SUMO as vehicle populations increase, demonstrating how computing architecture can expand the scale of agent-based traffic experiments. For our survey, this offers a practical way to investigate more demand conditions and congestion scenarios within a limited computing budget. However, the framework lacks some SUMO features, including adaptive traffic lights, so faster execution should not be treated as evidence of equivalent behavioral realism or control capabilities.

13. An agent-based urban simulation to assess the system-wide, spatial, and modal impacts of the deployment of an automated vehicle corridor with mixed traffic: a case study in New York City

Alnajjar, H. et al. 2024. An agent-based urban simulation to assess the system-wide, spatial, and modal impacts of the deployment of an automated vehicle corridor with mixed traffic: a case study in New York City. In Procedia Computer Science 238 (2024), 706–713. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procs.2024.06.081 .

Open-access full text and PDF.

Abstract Summary: This study uses an agent-based model of New York City to assess the wider effects of introducing an automated vehicle corridor in mixed traffic. The MATSim scenarios represent automation through changes in vehicle headways and road capacity, then examine resulting changes in traffic conditions, travel patterns, and mode choice. Comparisons between corridor scenarios show that additional capacity does not improve conditions uniformly across the network, with benefits in some locations accompanied by congestion elsewhere. The results indicate that corridor design must account for connecting roads and network-wide responses, while the tested scenarios produce relatively limited changes in mode choice.

Comments: Alnajjar and colleagues use MATSim to examine how a hypothetical automated-vehicle corridor would affect travel across New York City. Their scenarios produce benefits on some roads, including the Lincoln Tunnel, while increasing delays elsewhere, showing that corridor improvements can redistribute congestion across the network. This directly supports our survey’s NYC focus by demonstrating why a proposed intervention must be assessed beyond the roads it is designed to improve. Automated-vehicle effects are represented through capacity assumptions rather than detailed driving behavior, so the findings should be interpreted as scenario-dependent network responses.

14. Overcoming Data Scarcity in Calibrating SUMO Scenarios With Evolutionary Algorithms

Kappenberger, J. and Stuckenschmidt, H. 2025. Overcoming Data Scarcity in Calibrating SUMO Scenarios With Evolutionary Algorithms. In SUMO Conference Proceedings 6 (Jul. 2025), 133–148. https://doi.org/10.52825/scp.v6i.2590 .

Open-access full text and PDF.

Abstract Summary: This paper addresses the calibration of traffic simulations when only limited observations are available. The method combines routes derived from GPS data with sparse traffic counts and uses the CMA-ES evolutionary algorithm to adjust route probabilities supplied to SUMO’s routeSampler. Experiments on Mannheim scenarios evaluate whether the resulting simulated traffic better matches observed vehicle counts over different time periods. The approach improves agreement with those counts relative to the tested baseline, demonstrating a practical use of evolutionary optimization for demand calibration without establishing that the estimated routes uniquely reproduce actual traveler behavior.

Comments: Kappenberger and Stuckenschmidt combine GPS-derived routes with an evolutionary optimization method to calibrate SUMO demand against limited traffic counts in Mannheim. Their approach improves agreement between simulated and observed counts, providing a practical starting point when detailed origin–destination data are unavailable. This matters for our survey because misleading demand estimates can produce unrealistic bottlenecks and distort the predicted benefits of congestion interventions. However, matching counts at observed locations does not establish that the inferred routes are correct, and calibration on a single date leaves performance on other days uncertain.

15. A New Discrete Event Simulation of Large-Scale Car Traffic at Microscopic Level

Kieu, T. P. et al. 2025. A New Discrete Event Simulation of Large-Scale Car Traffic at Microscopic Level. In Proceedings of the 39th ACM SIGSIM Conference on Principles of Advanced Discrete Simulation (SIGSIM-PADS ’25), Santa Fe, USA. Association for Computing Machinery, New York, NY, USA, 143–153. https://doi.org/10.1145/3726301.3728409 .

Open-access full text and PDF.

Abstract Summary: This paper presents DEv-CF, a microscopic car-following model that updates vehicle states through discrete events rather than at fixed time intervals. Implemented in fwkDEVS, the model connects components for vehicle generation, road movement, and output collection, with vehicle objects managed within those components. Experiments compare its behavior and runtime with SUMO’s Intelligent Driver Model, including tests involving up to one million vehicle departures on a 20-kilometer road. DEv-CF runs faster on average and produces fewer output records in these benchmarks, although simplified acceleration and deceleration limit comparisons of behavioral detail.

Comments: Kieu and colleagues introduce DEv-CF, a car-following model that updates vehicle dynamics at relevant events rather than at every fixed time step. Implemented in fwkDEVS, it runs about 2.9 times faster than SUMO in the reported large-scale tests, but simplifies acceleration and deceleration and is evaluated on a single-road setting. The paper supports our survey by explaining how computational savings can be balanced against the detail needed to represent vehicle interactions underlying congestion. Its DEVS structure also provides a useful reference for a future DEVSJAVA implementation, although agreement with SUMO does not by itself validate the model against observed traffic.

16. Digital Twin-Aided Municipal Traffic Control

Pradhananga, R. et al. 2025. Digital Twin-Aided Municipal Traffic Control. In SUMO Conference Proceedings 6 (Jul. 2025), 149–161. https://doi.org/10.52825/scp.v6i.2619 .

Open-access full text and PDF.

Abstract Summary: This paper proposes DiTAT, a traffic-control system that uses camera observations to construct and update a simulated representation of an intersection. Vehicle detection and tracking provide inputs to a SUMO digital twin, which evaluates alternative signal settings and selects a configuration for a subsequent control interval. The study tests the approach using two recorded traffic videos from an intersection in Bellevue and compares outcomes across several decision intervals. Some adaptive configurations improve simulated throughput and travel times, with performance depending on the update interval and observed demand; the reported evaluation is based on recorded data rather than a live municipal deployment.

Comments: Pradhananga and colleagues combine vehicle detections from traffic videos with a SUMO digital twin that compares alternative signal settings for the next control interval. Their evaluation uses recordings from a Bellevue intersection and shows that selected adaptive settings can improve throughput and travel time relative to fixed timing. This connects directly to our survey by demonstrating how observed traffic can inform simulation-based decisions intended to relieve congestion as conditions change. However, the reported evaluation is limited to short recordings from one intersection, so it does not establish the benefits of continuous deployment across an urban network.

17. Advancing the MATSim Open Berlin Scenario: Improvements in transport scenario generation and calibration methods

Rakow, C. et al. 2025. Advancing the MATSim Open Berlin Scenario: Improvements in transport scenario generation and calibration methods. In Transportation Research Procedia 86 (2025), 732–739. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trpro.2025.04.091 .

Open-access full text and PDF.

Abstract Summary: This paper describes improvements to the open MATSim Berlin scenario, focusing on travel-demand generation, data integration, and calibration. The updated scenario incorporates revised activity patterns and commercial traffic, together with automated procedures for calibrating mode choice. Evaluation compares simulated outcomes with travel surveys, routing-based travel-time estimates, and stationary traffic counts. The changes improve agreement with observed mode shares and several travel distributions, although discrepancies remain in road traffic volumes, particularly where external and commercial travel demand are difficult to represent.

Comments: Rakow and colleagues improve the Open Berlin MATSim scenario through updated demand generation and automated calibration of transport-mode choices. The revised model closely matches survey mode shares and improves trip-distance distributions, but still underestimates traffic volumes, particularly on motorways, where some travel demand is incompletely represented. This is useful for our survey because reliable congestion analysis requires a credible baseline before changes to infrastructure or travel policy can be evaluated. The study also shows why calibration should use several kinds of evidence: matching mode shares does not necessarily mean that road volumes and travel times are equally accurate.

18. Autonomic Partition-Aware Malleable Microscopic Traffic Simulation

Siguenza-Torres, A. et al. 2025. Autonomic Partition-Aware Malleable Microscopic Traffic Simulation. In Proceedings of the 39th ACM SIGSIM Conference on Principles of Advanced Discrete Simulation (SIGSIM-PADS ’25), Santa Fe, USA. Association for Computing Machinery, New York, NY, USA, 132–142. https://doi.org/10.1145/3726301.3728412 .

Open-access full text and PDF.

Abstract Summary: This paper investigates how a distributed microscopic traffic simulation can adjust its computing resources as traffic volumes and spatial patterns change. The proposed CityMoS extension monitors workload and uses predictive cost models to select an allocation intended to maintain a target simulation pace. Its partition-aware model considers workload imbalance and vehicle migrations between computing partitions, with experiments on grid and non-grid networks. Resource savings depend on traffic patterns and adaptation overhead; snapshot and restart costs are measured in the evaluation but are not explicitly included in the adaptation policy.

Comments: Siguenza-Torres and colleagues extend CityMoS so that a distributed traffic simulation can adjust its computing resources as traffic volumes and movement patterns change. Their partition-aware cost model accounts for vehicles moving between computing partitions and responds more effectively to changing workloads than a simpler model based on vehicle and node counts. This supports our survey by addressing the practical computing demands of large congestion experiments with uneven traffic activity across a network. The benefits are constrained by the cost of saving and redistributing simulation state, so resource adaptation does not always reduce total computing effort.

19. Addressing Urban Traffic Congestion: A Deep Reinforcement Learning-Based Approach

Liu, T. 2025. Addressing Urban Traffic Congestion: A Deep Reinforcement Learning-Based Approach. Technical Report 25-13. Mineta Transportation Institute. https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/84808 .

Download & Read PDF version

Abstract Summary: This report investigates adaptive traffic control using deep reinforcement learning to reduce delays and improve vehicle movement through intersections. Its main approach combines deep Q-networks with an autoencoder that compresses traffic-state information, alongside parameter tuning and the use of average vehicle speed as a traffic-performance indicator. Simulation studies examine single-intersection control, multiple-intersection control, and a separate application involving left-turn movements and vehicle communication. The proposed approaches improve waiting-time or speed measures relative to the tested baselines, while their effectiveness in more complex conditions and with live traffic data remains to be established.

Comments: Liu investigates reinforcement-learning approaches to traffic management, including a Deep Q-Network signal controller paired with an autoencoder that compresses traffic-state information. In the reported single-intersection experiment, this combination reduces average waiting time from 340 seconds for the baseline to 212 seconds, providing a concrete example of the benefits of learned control in simulation. The report supports our survey by connecting the controller’s representation of traffic conditions with measurable congestion outcomes and offering a comparison with approaches that emphasize communication among signal agents. Its results still require testing with more varied traffic, pedestrians, and real-world observations before similar improvements can be expected in deployment.

20. Verification and Calibration of Microscopic Traffic Simulation Using Driver Behavior and Car-Following Metrics for Freeway Segments

Medina, J. C. et al. 2024. Verification and Calibration of Microscopic Traffic Simulation Using Driver Behavior and Car-Following Metrics for Freeway Segments. Technical Report FHWA-HRT-24-103. Federal Highway Administration, Office of Safety and Operations Research and Development. https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/74233 .

Download & Read PDF version

Abstract Summary: This report develops methods for checking and calibrating microscopic freeway simulations using observed driver behavior. Drawing on more than 1,600 hours of naturalistic driving data from over 1,700 drivers, it examines measures such as vehicle spacing, acceleration, and jerk across different speed ranges. The accompanying Naturalistic Assessments of Car-Following Trajectories tool compares simulated trajectories with empirical distributions to identify differences in modeled behavior. These measures supplement conventional checks based on traffic flow, travel times, and queues, providing a more detailed basis for assessing whether a simulation reproduces how individual drivers follow other vehicles.

Comments: Medina and colleagues use naturalistic driving data to establish reference distributions for vehicle spacing, acceleration, and changes in acceleration. Their comparison tool checks simulated trajectories against these observations, supplementing calibration based only on aggregate travel times, delays, and queues without automatically selecting model parameters. This strengthens our survey by providing a way to assess whether predicted congestion improvements depend on realistic vehicle interactions or on poorly calibrated driving behavior. The reference data cover passenger cars on freeways in daytime and good weather, so their application to urban intersections or other conditions requires further validation.
