Selected Papers and Annotations

Agent-based modeling and simulation for understanding and reducing urban traffic congestion.

20 distinct publications from the 60-entry bibliography: 4 foundational papers, 8 recent journal papers, and 8 conference papers or technical reports.

Paper categories

4 foundational papers 8 journal papers 6 conference papers + 2 reports

How can agent-based models evaluate congestion-reduction strategies while balancing behavioral realism, computational efficiency, and agreement with observed traffic?

4 foundational papers

1. An Agent-Based Microsimulation Model of Swiss Travel: First Results

Raney, B. et al. 2003. An Agent-Based Microsimulation Model of Swiss Travel: First Results. Networks and Spatial Economics 3, 1 (Jan. 2003), 23–41. https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022096916806 .

Read PDF version

Abstract Summary: This study represents individual travelers within a modular transport simulation and tests an initial implementation on Swiss morning traffic. Repeated route adjustment links individual decisions to network conditions, while parallel computing supports a large simulated population.

Comments: Raney and colleagues connect individual route choices with the traffic conditions those choices collectively produce, establishing a foundation for large-scale agent-based traffic modeling. The repeated route adjustment captures feedback between travelers and the network, which matters when evaluating interventions that change travel times or route attractiveness. The model also combines demand generation, routing, and parallel simulation, illustrating how behavioral representation and computational scale can be addressed together. Its initial focus on Swiss morning car traffic limits direct application to New York City's multimodal travel, but the feedback structure remains relevant to studying congestion. This paper fits the survey as a foundation for investigating whether changes to routes or road networks produce lasting congestion relief once travelers adjust their decisions.

2. A Distributed Approach for Coordination of Traffic Signal Agents

Bazzan, A. L. C. 2005. A Distributed Approach for Coordination of Traffic Signal Agents. Autonomous Agents and Multi-Agent Systems 10, 1 (Jan. 2005), 131–164. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10458-004-6975-9 .

Download & Read PDF version

Abstract Summary: Bazzan investigates coordinating traffic signals through interacting autonomous agents and evolutionary game-theoretic mechanisms. Traffic scenarios are used to examine whether distributed decisions can produce coordinated signal operation without relying on a central controller.

Comments: Bazzan treats traffic signals as autonomous agents that coordinate through evolutionary game-theoretic mechanisms. This approach addresses a central congestion problem: improving traffic flow at one intersection can increase queues and delays at neighboring intersections. The paper provides a foundation for comparing how distributed controllers balance local decisions with coordination across a road network. Its agents represent signal controllers, making its contribution to the survey primarily about traffic management rather than individual driver behavior. The paper directly fits the investigation of congestion reduction by examining how cooperation among signal agents can coordinate traffic movement across intersections, providing a foundation for comparison with more recent learning-based controllers.

3. Generating complete all-day activity plans with genetic algorithms

Charypar, D. and Nagel, K. 2005. Generating complete all-day activity plans with genetic algorithms. Transportation 32, 4 (Jul. 2005), 369–397. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11116-004-8287-y .

Read PDF version

Abstract Summary: This paper develops a genetic algorithm that maintains and improves alternative daily activity plans for individual travelers. A utility function evaluates plans, linking the benefits of activities with the travel needed to reach them.

Comments: Charypar and Nagel use genetic algorithms to generate and improve travelers’ daily activity plans, linking travel demand to the activities people want to complete. The utility-based approach represents travel as part of a broader scheduling decision, giving agent-based models a behavioral basis for when and why trips occur. This connection is relevant to congestion management because changes in travel costs or incentives can affect the timing and organization of travel. The paper’s main contribution is a method for constructing travel plans; the realism of those plans depends on the utility assumptions and constraints used to evaluate them. This paper fits the survey by supplying a behavioral basis for investigating demand-management measures, such as incentives to travel outside peak periods, although the paper itself does not establish the effectiveness of those measures.

4. Interactive hybrid simulation of large-scale traffic

Sewall, J. et al. 2011. Interactive hybrid simulation of large-scale traffic. ACM Transactions on Graphics 30, 6, Article 135 (Dec. 2011), 12 pages. https://doi.org/10.1145/2070781.2024169 .

Full text and PDF available through the DOI link above.

Abstract Summary: This paper couples detailed vehicle agents in selected areas with a faster aggregate traffic-flow model elsewhere in the network. Dynamic coupling allows large road networks to be simulated interactively, with demonstrations using real traffic data and synthetic scenarios.

Comments: Sewall and colleagues combine detailed vehicle agents with an aggregate traffic-flow model to make large road networks more practical to simulate interactively. This hybrid structure supports detailed analysis of selected locations while retaining the surrounding traffic that influences them. For congestion studies, it offers a way to examine bottlenecks without applying the same computationally expensive level of detail throughout the network. The approach also makes the transfer of traffic between representations an important consideration, since inconsistencies at their boundaries could affect simulated congestion. The paper fits the survey by offering a modeling approach for testing local congestion interventions while accounting for their wider effects, such as queues spreading onto surrounding roads.

Abidi, S. et al. 2025. Analyzing communication policies in cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning for traffic signal control: A simulation-based study. Simulation Modelling Practice and Theory 141, Article 103100 (May 2025). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.simpat.2025.103100 .

Abstract Summary: The study evaluates information-sharing policies in decentralized multi-agent reinforcement learning for traffic signal control. Its Co-DQN experiments examine how exchanged information affects controller learning and cumulative vehicle waiting time.

Comments: Abidi and colleagues examine how information sharing affects cooperation among reinforcement-learning agents that control traffic signals. Their Co-DQN experiments connect communication policies with controller learning and cumulative vehicle waiting time, making coordination a measurable part of congestion management. The study is useful for understanding how neighboring traffic information can inform decisions that would otherwise rely on local observations. It extends the survey’s treatment of distributed signal control by making the content and use of shared information central to the comparison of control strategies. This paper directly supports the survey’s investigation of congestion reduction by examining which communication policies help signal agents make coordinated decisions and reduce vehicle waiting.

6. RouteRL: Multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for urban route choice with autonomous vehicles

Akman, A. O. et al. 2025. RouteRL: Multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for urban route choice with autonomous vehicles. SoftwareX 31, Article 102279 (Sep. 2025). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.softx.2025.102279 .

Full text and PDF available through the DOI link above.

Abstract Summary: RouteRL is a software framework combining multi-agent reinforcement learning with microscopic traffic simulation for urban route-choice experiments. It supports interactions between behaviorally modeled human travelers and autonomous vehicles learning routing policies under different objectives.

Comments: RouteRL combines multi-agent reinforcement learning with microscopic traffic simulation to study route choice in traffic containing human travelers and autonomous vehicles. Its framework supports comparisons between modeled human decisions and learned routing policies, including policies with different individual or cooperative objectives. This makes it relevant to congestion research because routing decisions redistribute demand across roads and can shift delays between groups of travelers. Its main contribution is an experimental framework; the congestion benefits of a routing policy still depend on the behavioral assumptions, objectives, and traffic scenarios used. This paper fits the survey by providing a way to test whether cooperative route guidance reduces network-wide congestion more effectively than routing decisions that prioritize each vehicle’s own travel time.

7. Enhancing Travel Reservation Benefits Through Incentive and Penalty Mechanisms in Urban Congested Roads

Chen, H. et al. 2025. Enhancing Travel Reservation Benefits Through Incentive and Penalty Mechanisms in Urban Congested Roads. Applied Sciences 15, 3, Article 1393 (Jan. 2025). https://doi.org/10.3390/app15031393 .

Full text and PDF available through the DOI link above.

Abstract Summary: The authors combine agent-based optimization and dynamic traffic simulation to study incentives and penalties in a travel reservation system. Experiments on the Sioux Falls network examine heterogeneous traveler responses and report improvements in selected network and travel-cost measures.

Comments: Chen and colleagues investigate how incentives and penalties in a travel reservation system can influence demand on congested roads. Their combination of agent-based optimization and dynamic traffic simulation represents differences among travelers and examines their responses on the Sioux Falls network. The paper broadens the survey beyond signal timing and route guidance by showing how congestion management can target travelers’ decisions before traffic reaches a bottleneck. Its reported network and travel-cost benefits are relevant to demand management, although their practical significance depends on participation, compliance, and the costs of providing incentives. The paper directly fits the survey by investigating how a reservation policy can influence individual travel decisions to manage demand on congested roads, allowing comparison with strategies that control traffic after vehicles enter the network.

8. FAIRLANE: A multi-agent approach to priority lane management in diverse traffic composition

Dubey, R. K. et al. 2025. FAIRLANE: A multi-agent approach to priority lane management in diverse traffic composition. Transportation Research Part C: Emerging Technologies 171, Article 104919 (Feb. 2025). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trc.2024.104919 .

Full text and PDF available through the DOI link above.

Abstract Summary: FAIRLANE uses multi-agent reinforcement learning to manage access to priority lanes in mixed traffic containing connected autonomous, semi-autonomous, and human-driven vehicles. The framework considers traffic efficiency, comfort, and safety when agents learn how to use available lane space.

Comments: FAIRLANE applies multi-agent reinforcement learning to priority-lane management in traffic containing connected autonomous, semi-autonomous, and human-driven vehicles. It connects decisions about access to limited lane space with traffic efficiency, comfort, and safety. The paper fits the congestion topic by examining how road capacity can be allocated as different vehicle technologies share the same network. Its broader set of objectives also helps frame an important comparison: an intervention’s value depends on both overall performance and how its benefits are distributed among vehicle groups. The paper supports the survey’s investigation by treating lane access as a controllable decision, allowing assessment of whether adaptive allocation improves traffic flow while accounting for safety, comfort, and the needs of different road users.

9. Social implications of coexistence of CAVs and human drivers in the context of route choice

Jamróz, G. et al. 2025. Social implications of coexistence of CAVs and human drivers in the context of route choice. Scientific Reports 15, Article 6768 (Feb. 2025). https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-90783-w .

Full text and PDF available through the DOI link above.

Abstract Summary: This study models repeated route choice by human drivers and coordinated autonomous-vehicle fleets on a two-route network. Different fleet objectives can improve or worsen overall network outcomes and can advantage or disadvantage human travelers.

Comments: Jamróz and colleagues show how the objectives of coordinated autonomous-vehicle fleets influence route choice and the outcomes experienced by human drivers. Their two-route model demonstrates that fleet coordination can improve or worsen network performance, depending on the goal being optimized. This directly supports the congestion focus by distinguishing improvements for one group of road users from improvements for the network as a whole. The simplified network makes these interactions easier to interpret, while limiting direct generalization to a complex urban network such as New York City's network. This paper fits the survey by helping explain how the objectives assigned to routing agents can determine whether a proposed congestion solution reduces total delays or transfers them to other travelers.

10. Simplifying traffic simulation - from Euclidean distances to agent-based models

Khan, T. S. et al. 2024. Simplifying traffic simulation - from Euclidean distances to agent-based models. Computational Urban Science 4, Article 32 (Nov. 2024). https://doi.org/10.1007/s43762-024-00145-x .

Full text and PDF available through the DOI link above.

Abstract Summary: This study compares MATSim with faster shortest-path routing using GraphHopper as an approximation to more detailed traffic simulation. The comparison considers travel times, congestion, route similarity, and vehicle distance to assess when simplified calculations may be useful.

Comments: Khan and colleagues compare MATSim with faster routing approximations using GraphHopper, examining travel times, congestion, route similarity, and vehicle distance. The study addresses a practical modeling decision: how much simulation detail is needed to obtain useful results for a transportation problem. For congestion management, simplified methods can support initial screening, while interactions among travelers may require a more detailed model for evaluating particular interventions. Its contribution to the survey is a comparison of computational convenience with the traffic information retained or lost through simplification. The paper fits the survey by helping assess whether an inexpensive approximation is sufficient for comparing potential congestion solutions or whether a detailed agent-based model is needed to capture the interactions that determine their effectiveness.

11. Machine learning surrogates for agent-based models in transportation policy analysis

Natterer, E. S. et al. 2025. Machine learning surrogates for agent-based models in transportation policy analysis. Transportation Research Part C: Emerging Technologies 180, Article 105360 (Nov. 2025). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trc.2025.105360 .

Full text and PDF available through the DOI link above.

Abstract Summary: The authors develop a machine-learning surrogate for an agent-based transport model using graph and temporal representations. Experiments based on Paris MATSim scenarios predict road-level outcomes of capacity-reduction policies at a lower computational cost than repeated full simulation.

Comments: Natterer and colleagues develop machine-learning surrogates that approximate an agent-based transport model’s outputs at a lower computational cost. Their Paris MATSim experiments examine road-capacity reductions, connecting the method directly to the evaluation of transport policies. For congestion studies, this approach makes it more practical to screen numerous scenarios before selecting a smaller set for detailed simulation. A key limitation is that agreement with the source simulation does not establish agreement with observed traffic, and the surrogate can inherit the source model’s assumptions and errors. The paper fits the survey by supporting faster investigation of how road-capacity policies affect congestion, while illustrating why promising predictions still require confirmation through detailed simulation and appropriate traffic data.

12. FLAME-GPU for Traffic Systems: A Scalable Agent-Based Simulation Framework

Smilovitskiy, M. et al. 2025. FLAME-GPU for Traffic Systems: A Scalable Agent-Based Simulation Framework. Systems 13, 5, Article 376 (May 2025). https://doi.org/10.3390/systems13050376 .

Full text and PDF available through the DOI link above.

Abstract Summary: This paper presents a GPU-accelerated road-traffic agent model built with FLAME-GPU and compares its performance with CPU-based SUMO simulation. Experiments using the Isle of Wight road network investigate execution speed, vehicle population scale, and traffic representation.

Comments: Smilovitskiy and colleagues use FLAME-GPU to accelerate agent-based road-traffic simulation and compare its performance with SUMO on the Isle of Wight network. The paper addresses the computational demands of representing large vehicle populations and running repeated traffic experiments. This supports congestion research by making larger scenario comparisons and repeated runs more practical. Execution speed alone does not establish equivalent traffic realism, so the performance comparison must be interpreted alongside differences in hardware and behavioral representation. This paper fits the survey as a computational approach that could expand the range of congestion interventions and demand conditions researchers can test, supporting more extensive evaluation rather than proposing a traffic-control policy itself.

6 conference papers and 2 technical reports

13. An agent-based urban simulation to assess the system-wide, spatial, and modal impacts of the deployment of an automated vehicle corridor with mixed traffic: a case study in New York City

Alnajjar, H. et al. 2024. An agent-based urban simulation to assess the system-wide, spatial, and modal impacts of the deployment of an automated vehicle corridor with mixed traffic: a case study in New York City. In Procedia Computer Science 238 (2024), 706–713. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procs.2024.06.081 .

Full text and PDF available through the DOI link above.

Abstract Summary: This MATSim study evaluates a hypothetical mixed-traffic automated-vehicle corridor between the Staten Island Expressway and the Hugh L. Carey Tunnel. Reduced-headway scenarios alter modeled link capacity, and the reported comparison shows why additional corridor capacity can create congestion on connecting roads.

Comments: Alnajjar and colleagues use MATSim to evaluate a hypothetical automated-vehicle corridor linking the Staten Island Expressway and the Hugh L. Carey Tunnel. Their scenarios show that increasing corridor capacity can transfer congestion to connecting roads, highlighting the importance of evaluating effects across the wider network. This is especially relevant to the survey’s New York City focus because improvements within a corridor do not necessarily produce equivalent benefits for surrounding traffic. Automated-vehicle effects are represented through headway-related link-capacity assumptions, so the results concern network responses to those assumptions rather than detailed autonomous driving behavior. The paper directly fits the survey by evaluating a proposed congestion intervention in New York City and demonstrating how agent-based analysis can reveal unintended bottlenecks that a corridor-only assessment might overlook.

14. Overcoming Data Scarcity in Calibrating SUMO Scenarios With Evolutionary Algorithms

Kappenberger, J. and Stuckenschmidt, H. 2025. Overcoming Data Scarcity in Calibrating SUMO Scenarios With Evolutionary Algorithms. In SUMO Conference Proceedings 6 (Jul. 2025), 133–148. https://doi.org/10.52825/scp.v6i.2590 .

Full text and PDF available through the DOI link above.

Abstract Summary: The authors use the CMA-ES evolutionary algorithm to calibrate route probabilities for SUMO's routeSampler under limited observation availability. Experiments with the Mannheim SUMO Traffic Model report improved demand calibration over baseline methods for shorter and full-day scenarios.

Comments: Kappenberger and Stuckenschmidt use the CMA-ES evolutionary algorithm to calibrate route probabilities in SUMO when traffic observations are limited. Their Mannheim experiments address the difficulty of constructing realistic travel demand from incomplete data. This matters for congestion management because inaccurate demand can distort both the location of bottlenecks and the predicted benefits of an intervention. The paper strengthens the survey’s treatment of calibration, while sparse observations leave uncertainty about how well a fitted scenario represents traffic beyond the measured locations. This paper fits the survey by addressing a prerequisite for evaluating congestion solutions: constructing plausible traffic demand so that comparisons of signal timing, routing, or network changes are based on credible conditions.

Summary: The authors' reproducibility materials describe DEv-CF, a discrete-event car-following model implemented in Java using fwkDEVS. Experiments compare its vehicle behavior with the Intelligent Driver Model in SUMO and compare execution times as the simulated workload increases.

Comments: The authors’ reproducibility materials describe DEv-CF, a discrete-event car-following model implemented in Java using fwkDEVS. This paper fits the survey by informing how an event-based model could represent the vehicle interactions underlying queue formation and traffic flow, providing a possible basis for later congestion experiments rather than a demonstrated congestion-reduction strategy.

Download & Read PDF version

Abstract Summary: This FHWA report uses naturalistic freeway driving data to define calibration targets for vehicle spacing, acceleration, and jerk. It also presents a tool for comparing simulated car-following trajectories with observed behavior, supplementing conventional aggregate traffic calibration.

Comments: Medina and colleagues use naturalistic driving data to establish calibration targets for vehicle spacing, acceleration, and jerk in microscopic traffic simulation. Their trajectory-comparison tool supplements aggregate measures by examining whether simulated vehicle interactions resemble observed driving behavior. This matters for congestion studies because car-following assumptions influence queue formation and discharge, which can change the predicted effects of an intervention. The report strengthens the survey’s validation criteria, although its focus on freeway passenger vehicles in daytime and good weather limits direct transfer to other urban driving conditions. This paper fits the survey by providing a way to assess whether models used to investigate congestion solutions represent realistic driving behavior, helping distinguish credible predicted improvements from effects caused by poorly calibrated vehicle interactions.
